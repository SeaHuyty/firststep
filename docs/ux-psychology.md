# UX psychology: what each screen applies, and why

FirstStep's users are first-time investors who are a little scared of getting it wrong. Each principle below comes from published research or established design guidance, and each one turns into a concrete decision in the UI. Visual decisions (typefaces, type scale, spacing, radii, contrast) and their sources are in `docs/standards/ui-spec.md` in the project handoff.

**We use these principles only to help people decide well.** There is no urgency, no scarcity, no fake social proof and no celebration of trades: the brand rules ban them, and regulators criticised trading apps for gamifying novices.

| Principle | What the evidence says | What FirstStep does |
|---|---|---|
| **Hick's law, progressive disclosure** | Decision time grows with the number of choices [L1]. NN/g: show only the core options first and defer secondary ones; two levels is the practical maximum, and "the very fact that something appears on the initial display tells users that it's important" [N1]. | One question at a time, with at most 6 chips. One primary button per screen. Rule details ("Why?" cards) and chart tables sit one click away, never deeper. |
| **Goal-gradient and endowed progress** | People speed up as a goal gets closer (Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng 2006) [K1]. A task framed as already started gets finished more often: 34% vs 19% in Nunes and Drèze's car-wash field test [ND1]. | The step bar (Check, Profile, Goal, Plan, Confirm, Watch) is always visible and fills as you go. Each question shows "3 of 8". The first question is asked straight after the welcome, so the journey has already started. All progress shown is real; nothing is faked. |
| **Defaults** | Automatic enrolment raised 401(k) participation sharply, and many people kept the default rate and fund, partly reading the default as advice (Madrian and Shea 2001) [MS1]. | The default is always the suitable path: the model portfolio for the client's class, with a monthly plan pre-filled from their goal. A custom mix is possible only by asking Sprout, and the guardian checks it. Because defaults read as advice, ours are only the rule-checked plans. |
| **Myopic loss aversion** | Investors who check results often take less risk than suits them (Benartzi and Thaler 1995), and less frequent feedback raised risk-taking in experiments (Gneezy and Potters 1997) [BT1, GP1]. Later work questions whether loss aversion is the cause, but the frequency effect itself is the robust finding [GP2]. | The portfolio headline is the value against the money paid in since starting, never this month's change. The main fast-forward button moves a year. When markets drop, Sprout puts the drop in the plan's own timescale ("built for years, not months"). |
| **Framing outcomes** | Beginners can't judge advice quality; transparency raises trust most in exactly that case (Rühr, Berger and Hess 2021, in the handoff research). | Outcomes always show bad, typical and good cases in dollars, next to the money paid in, so the bad case is read against what you put in, not against the good case. A "Why?" sits beside every number. |
| **Positive friction** | Frictionless flows can leave people on autopilot; adding a confirmation step to a credit union's loan application reduced risk and confused calls (ideas42) [I1]. | Friction only where money is committed. The confirm dialog lists every order and needs "I understand the value can go down" ticked. Focus starts on Cancel, the least destructive option, as the WAI-ARIA dialog pattern advises [W1]. A big sale after a fall waits 24 hours (COOL-01). Everywhere else is one tap. |
| **Peak-end rule** | People judge an experience by its most intense moment and its end. In the cold-water study most people chose to repeat a longer painful trial because it ended slightly better (Kahneman et al. 1993) [KA1]. | The hard moment, "not yet", is turned into a plan with a date ("ready by Sep 2025"). Every journey ends on a calm, clear state: a receipt, or the next step of the savings plan. No confetti. |
| **Doherty threshold** | Productivity soars when the system responds in under about 400 ms, so neither side waits [L1]. | Your message appears at once. Chips and buttons disable and show what is happening ("Sprout is thinking…", then the steps it ran). Skeletons have the shape of the content, never a blank screen. |
| **Fitts's law** | Time to hit a target depends on its distance and size; larger targets mean fewer errors [L1]. | Every target is at least 44 px, primary buttons are 48 px, and a card's action sits inside the card it belongs to. On phones the chat input is pinned to the bottom, near the thumb. |
| **Change blindness** | People miss changes outside their focus; keep feedback where they are looking and close to the control that caused it, use motion as the cue, one change at a time (NN/g) [NN2]. | Every turn that changes the plan also puts one plain result line in the chat ("6 orders filled for $6,000, then $300 a month") with "See in plan". The plan pane scrolls to the single most important changed card. |
| **One-second spotlight** | A brief highlight on the area that just changed, fading within a second or two, lets people confirm the change and carry on (37signals' "yellow fade", 2004) [YF1]. | The changed plan card gets a 1.6-second ring that fades. |
| **Split workspace** | Chat-plus-workspace products (Claude Artifacts, ChatGPT Canvas) keep the conversation and the output side by side so neither pushes the other off-screen; small screens need their own mode [CA1]. | On desktop the chat and the plan scroll independently, with the message box pinned under the chat. Phones keep tabs, plus the plan cards inline in the chat. |
| **Typing indicator and response timing** | A typing indicator raises perceived social presence, especially for novices [TI1]. Short, length-scaled delays made chatbots feel more human and satisfying (Gnewuch et al. 2018), but delays put off experienced users (2022 follow-up) [GN1, GN2], and flow breaks after about 1 second (Nielsen) [NR1]. | Three pulsing dots while Sprout works; a 0.35–1 s length-scaled pause only when the reply came back faster; words appear at an even pace (about 0.6 s per reply), then cards rise in. Reduced-motion users get everything at once. |
| **Numeracy and icon arrays** | Less numerate people rely more on framing and feelings than on the figures (Peters et al. 2006) [PE1]. Icon arrays and "X out of 100" help them understand risk (Galesic, Garcia-Retamero and Gigerenzer 2009) [GG1]. | Results lead with words and dollars ("Most likely $58,016, $16,016 more than you put in"), and the chance of a first-year loss is shown as 100 dots ("21 out of 100 replays"). The year-by-year chart and fund details sit one click away. |
| **Concrete loss examples** | The FCA's behavioural testing found "your capital is at risk" fails to convey loss, especially for new investors; specific, behaviourally worded warnings with a "learn more" link raised comprehension by 6–10 points [F1]. Nutmeg shows its bad case in money with its odds [N1]. | "The honest part": what this mix did in its worst and best 12 months since 2000, in the person's own dollars, computed from real history up to the session's month. |
| **Structural assurance and trust** | People trust an unfamiliar system more when they can see the protective structures around them (McKnight, Choudhury and Kacmar 2002) [MK1]. For robo-advisors, competence and integrity drive trust (Schütz et al. 2023), and conversational warmth adds affective trust (Hildebrand and Bergner 2021) [RA1, RA2]. | "Your safeguards" sits at the top of the plan and ticks on as each protection applies: practice money only, savings and debts checked first, only funds at your level, money spread out, nothing bought without your Confirm, big sales after a drop wait, every step recorded. |
| **Companionship** | Ant Fortune's "帮你投" (with Vanguard) pairs the portfolio with ongoing notes and commentary, and reports that readers chased gains and panic-sold less (self-reported) [AF1]. | Each fast-forward ends with a short Sprout note in the person's own numbers ("It's now Mar 2020. Your plan is worth $7,180, and you've put in $7,500...") and calm context when markets fall. |
| **Clickability signifiers (welcome page)** | In NN/g's eyetracking study of 71 users, pages with weak signifiers took 22% longer and drew 25% more fixations, and weak cues cause "click uncertainty" [NN3]. Don't give static boxes button-like styling, and "people have difficulty picking out the clickable elements when similar-looking items compete with each other" [NN4]. A slight drop shadow is the card's clickability signifier [NN5]. One person's first impression of our old page: the bordered promise boxes looked clickable, did nothing, and taught them the demo boxes would not work either. | The promises are plain icon-and-text lines with no box. Only things you can press look pressable: the demo people are raised cards (one light source, shadow grows on hover, border turns green, "Try as Leo" with an arrow), and the answers are outlined pills. Nothing static has a border, shadow or fill that a button has. |
| **Value before sign-up (welcome page)** | Gradual engagement: let people use the product before any form (Wroblewski, "Sign up forms must die") [LW1]. Duolingo runs the first lesson before asking for an account [DU1]; Lemonade's Maya asks one plain question at a time in chat and gives the price before the email [LE1]. NN/g: show real examples of what the site does rather than describing it, and avoid visual competition among homepage elements [NN6]. | The welcome page *is* the first turn: Sprout types a greeting and asks the real first question ("How old are you?") with its answers. Tapping one starts the session with that answer saved and carries on in the chat, so the first click is already progress (endowed progress, above). "About 5 minutes" and "No sign-up, practice money only" sit under the answers to lower the cost of starting. There is no separate Start button to read past. |
| **Cognitive load and plain language** | GOV.UK writes for a reading age of 9; Monzo's rule is no unexplained jargon (handoff research). | Short sentences, one idea each. Jargon is a tappable term with a fixed definition. Numbers come from one formatter: money in dollars, changes signed with a true minus. |
